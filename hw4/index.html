Executive Summary & Research Directives

In security engineering, modeling adversarial penetration against distributed infrastructure requires treating attack campaigns as discrete-time stochastic processes. We consider a fleet of $m$ independent attackers sequentially targeting an enterprise attack surface of $n$ systems (or defense perimeters).

Each intrusion attempt generates an independent and identically distributed (I.I.D.) increment $X_i$, denoting the incremental penetration score. We investigate two fundamental stochastic formulations:

  • Bernoulli Model: $X_i \in \{0, 1\}$ with $P(X_i = 1) = p$, characterizing an asymmetric process where attackers either achieve incremental privilege ($+1$) or stall ($0$).
  • Rademacher Model: $X_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ with $P(X_i = +1) = P(X_i = -1) = 1/2$, formalizing a balanced, zero-mean tug-of-war where attacker advancement ($+1$) is countered by defensive remediation or honeypot mitigation ($-1$).

By simulating large trajectory ensembles across variable system depths $n$, this study investigates the asymptotic distribution and variance scaling of the Total Score $S_n$, the Average Score $S_n/n$ (Law of Large Numbers), and the Normalized Score $S_n/\sqrt{n}$ (Central Limit Theorem), complemented by formal Kolmogorov-Smirnov goodness-of-fit testing and the Berry-Esseen Theorem.

1. Stochastic Increment Models

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space. The penetration journey of an attacker across $n$ systems is modeled as a sum of I.I.D. random variables $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.

Property Bernoulli Model ($X_i \in \{0, 1\}$) Rademacher Model ($X_i \in \{-1, +1\}$)
Support & Probabilities $P(X_i = 1) = p, \quad P(X_i = 0) = 1 - p$ $P(X_i = +1) = 1/2, \quad P(X_i = -1) = 1/2$
Expected Value ($\mu$) $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot (1-p) = \mathbf{p}$ $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = (+1)\frac{1}{2} + (-1)\frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{0}$

Cybersecurity Context Unidirectional penetration: Successful exploit vs. failed attempt. Symmetric engagement: Attacker privilege escalation vs. SOC detection/remediation.

2. The Three Normalization Regimes & Variance Analysis

The core analytical question is how the aggregate score scales with system depth $n$. Dividing the sum $S_n$ by different powers of $n$ reveals distinct physical and probabilistic phenomena:

1. Total Score: $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$

Cumulative Breach Depth: Measures the raw penetration across the enterprise.

$\mathbb{E}[S_n] = n\mu$
$\text{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2 \implies \mathbf{O(n)}$

Scaling Behavior: Variance diverges linearly with $n$. The standard deviation $\sigma \sqrt{n}$ expands, meaning the envelope of potential attacker outcomes widens indefinitely as more systems are targeted.

2. Average Score: $S_n/n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$

Penetration Success Rate: The empirical breach frequency per system attacked.

$\mathbb{E}[S_n/n] = \mu$
$\text{Var}(S_n/n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \implies \mathbf{O(1/n) \to 0}$

Law of Large Numbers (LLN): Because variance decays to zero, Chebyshev's Inequality guarantees convergence in probability: $$P\left(\left|\frac{S_n}{n} - \mu\right| \ge \epsilon\right) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 0$$ All sample trajectories collapse ("funnel") into the constant horizontal expectation line $\mu$.

3. Normalized Score: $S_n/\sqrt{n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$

Diffusive Scaling: Balances cumulative drift against diffusive expansion.

$\mathbb{E}[S_n/\sqrt{n}] = \sqrt{n}\mu$
$\text{Var}(S_n/\sqrt{n}) = \sigma^2 \implies \mathbf{O(1) \text{ (Invariant)}}$

Central Limit Theorem (CLT): In the zero-mean Rademacher case ($\mu=0, \sigma^2=1$), the variance remains strictly equal to 1 for all $n$. By the Lindeberg-Lévy CLT, the terminal distribution converges to the standard Gaussian: $$\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Why Rademacher was Introduced: The Role of Zero-Mean Centering

Notice the crucial difference between the two models: for Bernoulli, $S_n/\sqrt{n}$ has expectation $\sqrt{n}p \to \infty$. The uncentered distribution drifts rapidly to the right. The Rademacher model was specifically introduced because its mean is naturally zero ($\mu = 0$). Thus, $S_n/\sqrt{n}$ is already centered and standardized without artificial shifting!

For Bernoulli, to observe the Central Limit Theorem bell curve centered at zero, one must apply the centered standardization $Z_n = \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, which our interactive suite supports via the checkbox below.

The Berry-Esseen Theorem: Quantitative Convergence Rate to Normality

The Central Limit Theorem asserts convergence in distribution as $n \to \infty$, but does not state how quickly the Gaussian shape is reached. The Berry-Esseen Theorem provides the exact non-asymptotic error bound between the normalized CDF $F_n(x)$ and the standard normal $\Phi(x)$:

$$\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} \left| F_n(x) - \Phi(x) \right| \le \frac{C \cdot \rho}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{n}}$$

where $\rho = \mathbb{E}[|X - \mu|^3]$ is the third absolute central moment and $C \le 0.4748$ is the Shevtsova constant.

  • In the Rademacher model, symmetry implies zero skewness ($\gamma_1 = 0$) and $\rho = 1$, yielding the rapid convergence rate $\approx \frac{0.47}{\sqrt{n}}$. At $n = 500$, the maximum discrepancy is bounded below $0.021$.
  • In the Bernoulli model with asymmetric $p \ne 0.5$, skewness is non-zero ($\frac{1-2p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}$), creating visible skewness on small $n$ before the $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ dampening takes effect.

5. Cybersecurity Interpretation: Systems ($n$) vs. Attackers ($m$)

Connecting the mathematical properties of stochastic walks to operational cybersecurity reveals profound engineering principles for defense architectures:

1. The Role of System Depth ($n$): Unmasking True Capability

In small infrastructure deployments ($n \le 10$), sample variance is high ($\sigma^2/n = 0.1$). A mediocre attacker with low exploitation skill ($p = 0.2$) may penetrate several systems in sequence purely through stochastic luck, creating false impressions of elite offensive capability.

Conversely, as enterprises expand their defense in depth to hundreds of layers ($n \ge 500$), the Law of Large Numbers eliminates luck. The penetration rate $S_n/n$ converges deterministically to the true underlying vulnerability density $p$. For defenders, increasing inspection depth $n$ forces adversaries into the asymptotic regime where their true exploit ceiling is strictly bound.

2. The Threat of Fleet Size ($m$): Extreme Value Theory

A standard defensive fallacy is to assume that because the average penetration score in a balanced defense (Rademacher model) is zero ($\mathbb{E}[S_n/n] = 0$), the network is safe. In enterprise defense, the vulnerability of the organization is determined not by the average attacker, but by the worst-case attacker in the fleet:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1 \le j \le m} S_n^{(j)}\right] \approx n\mu + \sigma \sqrt{2n \ln m}$$

According to Extreme Value Theory (EVT), the maximum of $m$ independent Gaussian variables scales as $\sigma \sqrt{2n \ln m}$. If an organization faces $m = 10,000$ concurrent automated botnet threats targeting $n = 500$ services:

$$\max_{1 \le j \le 10000} S_{500}^{(j)} \approx 1 \cdot \sqrt{2 \cdot 500 \cdot \ln(10000)} = \sqrt{1000 \cdot 9.21} \approx \mathbf{96 \text{ levels of breach!}}$$

The Asymmetric Defender Dilemma:

Even with a mathematically fair zero-mean defense where each individual system has a $50\%$ chance of deflecting an attack, an adversary pool of $m = 10,000$ attackers will almost certainly contain outliers penetrating deep into the internal network ($+96$ breach depth). Defenders must secure all systems against the maximum extreme statistic, whereas adversaries only require one statistical outlier to achieve catastrophic compromise.
