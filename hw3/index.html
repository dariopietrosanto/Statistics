Executive Summary & Research Directives

This project implements and formally evaluates two contrasting cryptographic paradigms: an Asymmetric Toy Cryptosystem built upon the secp256k1 elliptic curve defined over a small prime field $\mathbb{F}_{17}$, and a classical Symmetric Caesar Cipher in $\mathbb{Z}_{26}$.

Beyond providing working browser implementations of both ciphers, this paper investigates their mathematical mechanisms, key spaces, and statistical distributions. We demonstrate how Statistical Cryptanalysis (via Pearson's Chi-Square $\chi^2$ Goodness-of-Fit and N-gram Markov scoring) can recover Caesar keys from natural language redundancy, and contrast it with an Algebraic Key Recovery Attack that exploits the restricted scalar space of the toy elliptic curve.

1. The Toy Elliptic Curve Cryptosystem

Following the mathematical framework formulated in Homework 1, we utilize the secp256k1 Weierstraß equation $y^2 \equiv x^3 + 7 \pmod{17}$ with $a=0, b=7$ over the Galois field $\mathbb{F}_{17}$. The standard base generator is $G = (1, 5)$, which spans a cyclic subgroup of order $n=9$.

Algorithmic Stages and the Role of Keys

  • Key Generation (Receiver - Bob): Bob selects a private scalar $d_B \in [1, n-1]$. This scalar serves as Bob's secret trapdoor. Bob computes his Public Key point $Q_B = d_B \cdot G$ via scalar point multiplication. $Q_B$ is published openly.
  • Ephemeral Key Generation & Encapsulation (Sender - Alice): To achieve semantic security (IND-CPA), Alice does not use a static key. For each transmission, Alice generates a fresh, uniform pseudorandom ephemeral private nonce $k \in [1, n-1]$. Alice computes the ephemeral public point $C_1 = k \cdot G$.
  • Shared Secret Derivation & Encryption: Alice calculates the shared secret point $S = k \cdot Q_B = k \cdot (d_B \cdot G)$. Using the coordinates of $S$, Alice derives an algebraic stream mask $K = ((x_S \cdot 7 + y_S \cdot 11) \bmod 25) + 1$, and produces the ciphertext string $C_2$. The complete transmitted message is the tuple $(C_1, C_2)$.
  • Decapsulation & Decryption (Bob): Upon receiving $(C_1, C_2)$, Bob evaluates $S = d_B \cdot C_1 = d_B \cdot (k \cdot G) = k \cdot (d_B \cdot G)$. Because scalar multiplication is commutative over abelian groups, Bob obtains the identical shared point $S$ without ever learning Alice's nonce $k$. Bob computes the inverse mask and recovers the exact plaintext.

2. The Caesar Cipher

The Caesar cipher is an archetypal monoalphabetic substitution cipher. Operating on the residue ring $\mathbb{Z}_{26}$, each character $p_i \in \{0, \dots, 25\}$ of a plaintext message is mapped to a ciphertext character $c_i$ via a constant linear translation parameter $k \in \mathbb{Z}_{26}$:

Encryption:   $c_i = (p_i + k) \pmod{26}$  |  Decryption:   $p_i = (c_i - k) \pmod{26}$

3. Rigorous Comparative Analysis: Toy ECC vs. Caesar Cipher

While both schemes allow two parties to securely communicate across an unshielded channel, their fundamental architectures, statistical profiles, and security assurances belong to entirely different cryptographic epochs.

Architectural Dimension Caesar Cipher ($\mathbb{Z}_{26}$) Toy Elliptic Curve Cryptosystem ($\mathbb{F}_{17}$)
Cryptographic Paradigm Symmetric: A single shared secret key $k$ must be securely exchanged prior to communication. Asymmetric (Public-Key): Bob distributes $Q_B$ openly; Alice encrypts with zero prior secret agreement.
Key Space Size ($|\mathcal{K}|$) $|\mathcal{K}| = 26$ (effectively 25 non-trivial shifts). Trivial to exhaust manually in seconds. $|\mathcal{K}| = 9$ (in this toy demo) vs. $|\mathcal{K}| \approx 2^{256} \approx 1.15 \times 10^{77}$ in production secp256k1.
Encryption Nature Deterministic: Encrypting the plaintext character 'A' with key $k$ always yields the exact same ciphertext character. Probabilistic (IND-CPA): Encrypting 'A' with different ephemeral nonces $k$ yields radically different points $C_1, S$.
Mathematical Primitive Elementary modular arithmetic (Translation group in cyclic ring $\mathbb{Z}_{26}$). Nonlinear abelian group law over projective algebraic curves (Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem).
Statistical Invariance 100% Preserved: Letter frequencies, bigrams, and trigrams are merely rotated. $I(M; C) > 0$. Completely Destroyed: When ephemeral nonces vary, ciphertext symbols follow a uniform pseudo-random distribution.

Statistical Entropy & Frequency Invariance

The fatal flaw of the Caesar cipher is its lack of diffusion: the probability distribution of characters in the ciphertext is identical to that of the plaintext, shifted rigidly by an offset $k$. If the letter E is the most frequent character in an English plaintext, the character representing $\mathbf{E} + k \pmod{26}$ will remain the single most frequent symbol in the ciphertext.

In contrast, asymmetric schemes use ephemeral random nonces ($k$). Encrypting the word "BANANA" with ECC ElGamal or ECIES causes each 'A' to map to a different, statistically independent point on the curve. This achieves Ciphertext Indistinguishability under Chosen-Plaintext Attack (IND-CPA).

4. Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

Because natural languages possess high redundancy and non-uniform symbol distributions, a ciphertext produced by a monoalphabetic substitution cipher can be cracked deterministically without knowing the key.

The Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Goodness-of-Fit Criterion

Let $\mathbf{p} = [p_A, p_B, \dots, p_Z]$ represent the standardized expected frequency vector of the English language (e.g., $p_E \approx 0.127$, $p_T \approx 0.091$). For a ciphertext of length $N$, we test all 26 possible shift candidates $s \in \{0, \dots, 25\}$. For each candidate $s$, we decrypt the text, count the observed occurrences of each letter $O_c(s)$, and compute the expected count $E_c = N \cdot p_c$.

The Pearson's Chi-Square statistic measures the divergence between observed and expected frequencies:

$$\chi^2(s) = \sum_{c='A'}^{\text{'Z'}} \frac{\left(O_c(s) - E_c\right)^2}{E_c}$$

The candidate key $\hat{k}$ that minimizes $\chi^2(s)$ corresponds to the hypothesis whose letter distribution matches natural English with the highest probability.

Empirical Behavior across Sample Lengths & Limitations

1. The Law of Large Numbers (LLN) and Central Limit Theorem

As the ciphertext length $N \to \infty$, the sample variance $\sigma^2 = p_c(1 - p_c)/N$ approaches zero. On texts with $N \ge 200$, the Chi-Square statistic achieves virtually 100% accuracy, unambiguously identifying the true shift key.

Conversely, for short texts ($N < 20$), the sample distribution suffers from severe stochastic fluctuation (the "Law of Small Numbers"). In sentences like "MEET ME AT NOON", letters like 'E' and 'O' are over-represented while letters like 'T' or 'A' may be absent. Here, unigram $\chi^2$ can fail, whereas Bigram Scoring succeeds by evaluating adjacent transitional pairs (e.g., "TH", "HE", "IN").

2. Pathological Cases & Vulnerabilities of Frequency Analysis

Frequency analysis completely collapses in three situations: (a) Lipograms (literary texts intentionally written without specific letters, such as Ernest Vincent Wright's 50,000-word novel Gadsby which contains zero instances of the letter 'E'); (b) High-entropy inputs (encrypted files, compressed archives, or compiled binaries); (c) Polyalphabetic ciphers (such as the Vigenère cipher or Enigma, which alternate between multiple shifting keys).

5. Cryptanalysis of the Toy ECC Scheme: Exploiting Restricted Key Spaces

In Part 1 of this curriculum, the secp256k1 toy curve was constructed over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$ with $G = (1, 5)$. The resulting cyclic subgroup order is $n = 9$. Because this domain was purposely truncated to make manual calculation feasible, it introduces a fatal vulnerability to Discrete Logarithm Key Recovery.

Implementation of the Key Recovery Attack

An eavesdropper (Eve) who intercepts the Public Key $Q_B = d_B \cdot G$ and ciphertext $(C_1, C_2)$ does not need to inspect the ciphertext statistics. Eve can directly solve the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) by computing successive scalar multiplications $1 \cdot G, 2 \cdot G, 3 \cdot G, \dots$ until reaching $Q_B$.

Fundamental Contrasts: Caesar Statistical Attack vs. ECC Algebraic Attack

Property Caesar Cryptanalysis (Statistical) Toy ECC Attack (Algebraic / ECDLP)
Attack Surface Ciphertext-Only: Exploits statistical redundancy and non-uniform symbol frequencies of natural language. Public-Key / Key-Space Bounded: Exploits the small mathematical group order $n$. Completely ignores ciphertext text.
Data Dependency Requires large volumes of text ($N > 100$). Completely fails if the plaintext is random bytes or compressed. Independent of plaintext semantics. Succeeds instantaneously even if the message is pure random white noise.
Computational Complexity $O(26 \cdot N) \approx O(N)$ linear scans over the ciphertext string. $O(n)$ group operations for brute force, or $O(\sqrt{n})$ using Pollard's Rho or Baby-Step Giant-Step (BSGS).

Why the Same Attack is Computationally Infeasible Against Production ECC

In real-world blockchain protocols and financial systems, secp256k1 is defined over a 256-bit prime field:

$p = 2^{256} - 2^{32} - 977$  |  Group Order: $n \approx 1.15792089 \times 10^{77} \approx 2^{256}$

Against production secp256k1, solving the ECDLP cannot be performed by simple enumeration:

  • Exhaustive Search: Requires $O(n) \approx 2^{256}$ scalar point additions.
  • Pollard's Rho Algorithm: The most efficient known classical algorithm for generic elliptic curves requires $O(\sqrt{n}) \approx \sqrt{2^{256}} = 2^{128}$ point additions.

Thermodynamic & Physical Constraints (Landauer's Principle)

To understand the magnitude of $2^{128} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$ operations, consider a hypothetical quantum-efficient supercomputer capable of performing $10^{18}$ point operations per second (one quintillion ops/sec).

The time required to compute $2^{128}$ operations would be:

$$\frac{3.402 \times 10^{38}}{10^{18} \text{ ops/sec}} \approx 3.402 \times 10^{20} \text{ seconds} \approx 1.07 \times 10^{13} \text{ years}$$

Given that the estimated age of the universe is approximately $1.38 \times 10^{10}$ years, attacking production secp256k1 would require nearly 1,000 times the entire age of the cosmos. Furthermore, by Landauer's Principle ($E \ge k_B T \ln 2$), dissipating the heat generated by $2^{128}$ bit operations would require more energy than is consumed by our entire planet in multiple millennia.

Optional: Inherent Weaknesses in Toy Scheme Design vs. Production Standards

Beyond the restricted key space, several architectural vulnerabilities exist in naive toy implementations:

  1. Malleability of Plain ElGamal: In basic ElGamal schemes, the ciphertext point $C_2 = M + kQ_B$ is malleable. An active attacker can intercept the transmission and compute $C_2' = C_2 + \Delta$, causing the decrypted plaintext to alter predictably ($M' = M + \Delta$) without triggering an error. Production schemes resolve this via ECIES using HMAC or Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD) like AES-GCM.
  2. Catastrophic Ephemeral Nonce Reuse ($k_1 = k_2$): If Alice reuses the exact same ephemeral scalar $k$ to encrypt two distinct messages $M_1$ and $M_2$, both ciphertexts share identical public points $C_{1,1} = C_{1,2} = kG$. An observer immediately discovers that $C_{2,1} - C_{2,2} = M_1 - M_2$, completely exposing the algebraic difference between the plaintexts without recovering Bob's private key.
  3. Small Subgroup Attacks: If a curve order $N$ has small prime factors, an adversary can query public keys restricted to small subgroups (Pohlig-Hellman algorithm). Real secp256k1 specifically avoids this by having a prime order with cofactor $h = 1$.
